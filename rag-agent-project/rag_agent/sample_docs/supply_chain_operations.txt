Supply Chain Operations: Demand Planning Through Reverse Logistics

The operational supply chain behind an e-commerce brand runs through five
connected stages, and a failure at any stage shows up as a symptom at a
later one, which is why root-cause analysis in this business usually means
tracing a visible problem (a stockout, excess inventory) backward through
the chain rather than treating it as isolated.

Demand planning forecasts future sales using historical velocity,
seasonality, and planned promotions, and produces the single number
(forecasted demand) that every downstream decision depends on. Forecast
error compounds: an under-forecast leads to stockouts and lost Buy Box
share that takes weeks to recover even after inventory arrives; an
over-forecast leads to excess inventory, long-term storage fees, and
eventual markdown or liquidation.

Procurement translates the forecast into actual purchase orders, factoring
in supplier lead time, minimum order quantities, and payment terms.
Reorder point and safety stock calculations live here: the reorder point
determines WHEN to place an order, and safety stock determines how large a
buffer protects against demand or lead-time variability. A supplier with a
long or inconsistent lead time (common with overseas manufacturing) pushes
every other stage's buffers higher, which ties up working capital.

Inbound logistics moves purchased goods from the supplier to a
fulfillment center or warehouse, and this is where Incoterms (FOB, DDP,
EXW) determine who is responsible for freight, insurance, and customs at
each leg. Inbound shipment planning for Amazon FBA specifically requires
matching shipment plans to fulfillment center assignments, which Amazon
controls and which can split one shipment across multiple FCs,
complicating lead-time tracking.

Warehousing and fulfillment cover storage, pick/pack, and the actual
shipment to the end customer -- for FBA sellers this is largely outsourced
to Amazon, but 1P vendor and DTC channels often require the brand (or its
3PL) to manage this directly, with different cost structures and SLAs.

Reverse logistics handles returns, which in some categories (apparel
especially) can exceed 20-30% of units shipped. A returned unit's
condition determines whether it goes back into sellable inventory,
requires inspection/repackaging, or gets liquidated -- and reverse
logistics cost is frequently underestimated in margin planning because it
is tracked separately from the original fulfillment cost rather than
netted against the sale.

The common thread across all five stages is that supply chain performance
is measured in lagging indicators (in-stock rate, IPI score, days of
supply) that reflect decisions made weeks or months earlier, which is why
mature operations track leading indicators -- forecast accuracy, supplier
lead-time variance, PO on-time rate -- rather than waiting for a stockout
to reveal a planning problem.
