Customer Experience, Reviews, and Returns Policy

Customer experience on a marketplace is measured and defended differently
than in a DTC store, because the marketplace itself (not the brand) owns
the primary customer relationship and sets the rules the brand has to
operate inside.

Account health metrics are the marketplace's own scorecard for a seller:
Order Defect Rate (ODR, combining negative feedback, A-to-Z claims, and
chargebacks), Late Shipment Rate, Valid Tracking Rate, and Customer Voice
(VOC), which aggregates recent customer sentiment signals into a health
flag independent of the star rating shown on the listing itself. These
thresholds are enforced automatically -- cross them and a seller can lose
the ability to list new products or, at the extreme, have the account
suspended, regardless of overall sales performance.

Review management operates under strict marketplace rules: a seller
cannot ask a customer to change or remove a negative review, cannot offer
compensation in exchange for a review, and cannot review-gate (only
request reviews from customers believed to be satisfied). The compliant
lever is a generic post-purchase review request sent to every customer
through the marketplace's own request-a-review mechanism, plus genuinely
fixing the product or listing issue driving negative reviews in the first
place -- reviews are a lagging indicator of product and listing quality,
not something to be separately "managed" in isolation from fixing what
they're actually complaining about.

Return policy has both a marketplace-mandated floor and brand-level
choices above it. Marketplaces typically require accepting returns within
a minimum window (commonly 30 days) for most categories, with limited
exceptions (certain consumables, hazardous materials, final-sale items
clearly marked at purchase). Above that floor, a brand can choose to offer
a longer window or a more generous policy (no-questions-asked returns, a
prepaid return label on every order) as a competitive differentiator,
trading a higher return-processing cost for better reviews and repeat
purchase rate.

The customer-facing return policy and the operational reverse-logistics
process are two different things that should stay connected: the policy
is what the customer is promised (how many days, who pays return
shipping, refund vs. exchange), while reverse logistics is how a returned
unit's condition is actually assessed and routed once it comes back. A
generous customer-facing policy with a slow or under-resourced reverse
logistics process creates a gap where customers are promised fast refunds
the operations team can't actually deliver, which shows up as support
escalations and VOC complaints that look like a service problem but
actually originate from an operations capacity problem.

Escalation handling for customer complaints typically tiers by severity:
routine return/refund requests handled through standard self-service
flows; product safety or quality complaints routed to a dedicated review
queue regardless of volume, since these carry account-health and
liability risk disproportionate to their frequency; and complaints that
reference a policy violation (counterfeit claims, IP complaints) routed
to a compliance-specific escalation path rather than general customer
service, since these need documentation and a different response
standard than a routine complaint.
