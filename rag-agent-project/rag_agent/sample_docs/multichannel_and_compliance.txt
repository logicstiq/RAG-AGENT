Multi-Channel Operations and Cross-Border Tax Compliance

Running a brand across several marketplaces at once means the same
product often needs a genuinely different operational setup per channel,
not just a copy-pasted listing -- and cross-border selling adds a tax
compliance layer that exists independently of any single marketplace's
rules.

Walmart Marketplace shares conceptual ground with Amazon 3P (seller-owned
inventory, seller-set pricing) but runs its own content standards, its
own fulfillment program (Walmart Fulfillment Services, WFS, functionally
similar to FBA but a separate inventory pool), and its own performance
scorecard with different thresholds and consequences than Amazon's
account health metrics. A seller operating both channels typically keeps
separate safety stock and reorder calculations per channel rather than
pooling inventory, since a stockout on one channel doesn't free up units
sitting in the other channel's fulfillment network in time to matter.

TikTok Shop operates on a fundamentally different discovery model: sales
are driven by short-form video and livestream commerce rather than search
intent, so demand is far spikier and less forecastable from historical
velocity alone -- a single viral video can move more units in a day than
a month of steady-state sales, which breaks a reorder point calculation
built on average daily demand. Brands active here typically run thinner
per-SKU inventory commitments and lean more on fast replenishment lead
times than on large safety stock buffers, accepting a higher stockout
risk in exchange for not overcommitting capital to a demand curve that
can be driven by a single piece of content.

A DTC storefront (Shopify or similar) is the one channel where the brand
controls content, pricing, and the full customer data relationship with
no marketplace rules in between -- but it also means the brand owns
traffic generation (search, social, email, paid acquisition) entirely on
its own, with no marketplace search traffic to lean on. Fulfillment here
is either self-managed, run through a 3PL, or increasingly fulfilled from
the same inventory pool as a marketplace channel (Amazon's
Multi-Channel Fulfillment lets Amazon-held inventory also fulfill DTC
orders), which simplifies inventory planning but ties DTC fulfillment
reliability to that marketplace's own operational performance.

Cross-border tax compliance exists on top of all of this and does not
go away just because a marketplace handles the transaction. VAT
(Value-Added Tax, used across the EU and UK) generally must be collected
and remitted based on where the end customer is located, not where the
seller or warehouse is based; many marketplaces now collect and remit VAT
automatically on behalf of third-party sellers for consumer sales under
certain thresholds, but the seller remains responsible for registering
for a VAT number in any country where it holds inventory in a local
fulfillment center, since storing goods there typically creates a taxable
presence regardless of where sales happen. In the US, state sales tax
follows a similar split: most marketplaces now collect and remit sales
tax on marketplace transactions directly (marketplace facilitator laws),
but a seller with its own DTC store is usually responsible for its own
state-by-state sales tax registration and remittance wherever it has
nexus (a physical or economic presence over a state's threshold).

The common failure mode across channels is treating tax and compliance
as a one-time setup rather than something that changes whenever inventory
placement, channel mix, or sales volume changes -- storing inventory in a
new country's fulfillment center, or crossing an economic nexus threshold
in a new US state, can create a new filing obligation that nobody
notices until a mismatch surfaces in an audit.
