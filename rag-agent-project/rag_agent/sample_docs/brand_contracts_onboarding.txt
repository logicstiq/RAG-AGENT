Brand Contracts and Onboarding

When a brand management company takes on a new brand, the engagement
starts with a contract that defines scope, commercial terms, and
operational boundaries, and the quality of onboarding execution afterward
determines how quickly that brand becomes profitable to manage.

Contract structures vary, but the most common commercial models are:
a percentage-of-revenue fee (the management company takes a cut, often
10-25%, of net sales across the channels it manages); a hybrid model
combining a smaller revenue percentage with a flat monthly retainer
covering advertising management, content, and operations; or, less
commonly, a wholesale/distributor model where the management company
actually buys inventory from the brand and resells it, taking on
inventory risk directly in exchange for a larger margin capture. Contracts
typically specify which channels are in scope (Amazon 1P, 3P, Walmart,
TikTok Shop, DTC), minimum term length, termination notice period, and
IP/content ownership (who owns photography, copy, and A+ content created
during the engagement after the contract ends).

Before a contract is even drafted, a new brand or market opportunity
typically goes through a staged evaluation: an initial fit/scorecard
assessment (does this brand's category, price point, and current
performance make sense for the partnership at all), a financial model
(what commercial terms would make the engagement worthwhile for both
sides, given the brand's margin structure and volume), a negotiation
pass to pulse-check the brand's comfort with those terms, a feasibility
study (a concrete plan for how the management company would actually
grow the business in the target channel or market), and only then the
legal contract itself. Skipping the feasibility study to move faster is a
common mistake, because it means the first real growth plan gets built
*after* the commercial terms are already locked in, rather than informing
them.

Onboarding a new brand typically follows a staged sequence, often spanning
two to three months end to end for a new marketplace or region:

1. Intake and brand setup (roughly the first two weeks): collecting the
   initial SKU/product list from the brand, confirming which products
   will actually launch (a full catalog or a curated top-seller subset),
   and running compliance review in parallel -- regulatory requirements
   vary sharply by destination market, so this step cannot be skipped
   even under timeline pressure. A supplements or VMS (vitamins, minerals
   and supplements) brand expanding into a new country, for example, may
   need country-specific product registration (e.g. a government
   ingredient/safety approval) before a listing can go live at all, and
   that approval alone can take anywhere from 60 days to 6 months --
   meaning compliance timeline, not catalog work, is often the true
   critical path for a new-market launch. Packaging and labeling
   requirements also vary by destination (bilingual labeling, specific
   mandatory disclosures) and need to be checked against the new market's
   rules before products ship, not after.

2. Data preparation across systems (another one to two weeks): registering
   products in the content/PIM system, translating and localizing
   attributes and listing content for the destination market, selecting
   the correct category, and syndicating that data out to the marketplace
   itself, followed by a verification pass confirming the live listing
   actually matches what was submitted. A decision made here with lasting
   consequences: whether to reuse an existing ASIN/product ID across
   regions or create a new one per market. Reusing one lets a new
   market's listing inherit existing reviews, but it also means a
   suppression or policy issue in one market can take down the listing
   everywhere it's used -- new, market-specific IDs are generally the
   safer default despite losing that review history.

3. Inventory planning and inbound flow (commonly the longest phase, one to
   two months): handing off to demand planning with the historical
   velocity or launch-volume assumptions the plan needs, generating and
   confirming the purchase order, managing the brand's inbound shipment to
   a receiving warehouse, and then the outbound leg to wherever the
   marketplace's own fulfillment network requires the stock to physically
   sit -- which, for a cross-border or new-region launch, can itself take
   several weeks and is frequently the actual bottleneck between a
   finished listing and a sellable one.

4. Pricing architecture: setting an initial price (often deliberately
   high relative to competitors at first), then a defined cadence for
   testing price reductions against conversion and margin targets, with
   guardrails on how far price can move before requiring approval.

5. Ongoing communications and reporting cadence: a regular (commonly
   weekly or biweekly) reporting rhythm to the brand covering price tests,
   promotions, stockout risk, and advertising performance, plus a
   separate, often more frequent internal cadence among the operating
   team -- and a defined escalation playbook for the issues that come up
   repeatedly: listing suppression, IP or counterfeit complaints,
   unauthorized third-party sellers undercutting price, and inventory or
   logistics exceptions, each with its own typical root causes and a
   known internal and external escalation path rather than an ad hoc one
   worked out after the fact.

The most common onboarding failure mode is treating it as purely an
account-access handoff rather than a diagnostic and compliance process:
skipping the catalog and inventory audit, or underestimating how long
destination-market compliance actually takes, means problems that existed
before the engagement (stranded ASINs, an already-damaged IPI score, a
regulatory approval nobody budgeted time for) get misattributed to the new
management team's performance in the window new engagements are judged on.

Renewal and expansion conversations go more smoothly when the original
contract's success metrics were specific and were tracked consistently
from day one -- revenue growth, TACoS (total advertising cost of sale),
IPI score, and in-stock rate are the four metrics most commonly cited in
both onboarding plans and renewal reviews.
